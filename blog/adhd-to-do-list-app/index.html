ADHD to-do list app: what to look for, 5 to try

By one-by.one · Updated October 30, 2026

A good ADHD to-do list app shows you one next step instead of the whole pile, makes time visible with timers, takes little setup, and does not punish you when you fall behind. Those are our own criteria, not a medical standard. This guide explains why long lists stall many people, checks Tiimo, Structured, Todoist, Goblin Tools and Things against their own pages, and says where our app, one-by.one, fits. one-by.one is not a medical app.

Why long to-do lists stall people with ADHD

Two phrases come up whenever people talk about this. “Task paralysis” is the experience of staring at a list, knowing everything on it matters, and not starting any of it. “Time blindness” is losing track of how much time has passed, or how much is left. Both are informal names for things many people with ADHD describe, and neither is a test or a diagnosis. If you recognise them, you are not alone, and it is not a character flaw.

We are not clinicians, so here is what two non-commercial sources actually say:

  • The UK’s NHS lists, among the signs of ADHD in adults, “finding it hard to organise your time” and finding it hard to follow instructions or finish tasks. It also says that if symptoms affect your work, studies or relationships, you can book a GP appointment to find out what support is available. See the NHS page on ADHD in adults.
  • CHADD, a US non-profit for people with ADHD, published an article describing time blindness as consistent difficulty with perceiving elapsed or remaining time and sequencing events. It does not present it as a formal diagnosis. The strategies it lists include timers and alarms, visual schedules such as planners or apps, and breaking larger tasks into smaller ones. See CHADD: Time Unbound.

Neither source recommends a particular app, and neither do we make claims about what an app can do for you. What we can say is how an ordinary to-do list tends to feel when you are in this spot. Every item is visible at once, so the first job is choosing, and choosing is the hard part. Items carry over from yesterday and start to feel like a verdict. A list says what to do but not when it will end, so time stays invisible. A to-do app built around those problems looks different from one built around storing everything.

What makes a to-do list app ADHD friendly?

Search for an ADHD friendly to-do list app and you will find long feature lists. These are the four things we would check first.

  1. One next step. The app should be able to show a single thing to do now, not just a list to scan. If it only offers a list, look for a Today view you can keep short.
  2. Time you can see. A visible timer or timeline answers “how long has this been going?” without you having to track it. Timers that handle waiting (laundry, a meeting, a download) are even better, because waiting is where people lose the thread.
  3. Low setup. Apps that need a daily ritual of tagging, sorting and scheduling can become one more task. Look for fast capture, or for something that breaks a big item into steps for you.
  4. Forgiving when you fall behind. You will miss days. The app should let you reschedule, skip or clear what is left without a wall of red overdue items. This is our view, but we would be wary of anything that turns a missed day into a failure.

No app does all four equally well. Most of the choice is about which one you can live without.

Tiimo

Tiimo calls itself a “visual AI planner” and says it was made with ADHD and autism in mind. Its home page lists a color-coded visual planner, a to-do list with a brain-dump feature that turns thoughts into steps, a focus timer with a visible countdown, widgets and Live Activities, and an Apple Watch app. The page says Pro adds AI task breakdown, custom widgets and advanced scheduling. Tiimo’s pricing page offers a 7-day trial on yearly plans only, and does not state a dollar price; you see it at checkout. We did not evaluate the research claims on its page.

What Tiimo does better than us: it is a complete app on its own, with visual planning, Android and web versions, and no AI account needed. If you want to build the day yourself, visually, it is a strong candidate.

Structured

Structured puts tasks, calendar events and routines on a timeline. Its App Store listing markets it to people with ADHD, busy professionals and students. The free features include the drag-and-drop timeline, an inbox, sub-tasks, a Pomodoro focus timer, widgets and Live Activities. Pro adds calendar and Reminders integration, recurring tasks, AI day planning, custom notifications, and Replan, which reschedules missed tasks. The listing shows several different Pro prices, so we are not quoting one.

What Structured does better: Replan speaks directly to the “forgiving” criterion, and the free tier covers a lot. It also runs on more Apple devices than we do.

Todoist

Todoist is a general task manager rather than an ADHD tool; its home page does not mention ADHD. It has a Today view, reminders, recurring tasks and an AI assistant called Todoist Assist. Personal use is free, and its plans FAQ lists Pro at $7 a month or $60 a year, with a 7-day trial.

What Todoist does better: its free plan covers recurring tasks and up to five active personal projects, in list or board layouts, on every device; paid plans add calendar layouts and time blocking. The catch is that it is still built around lists. Keeping Today short is up to you, which is the part some people find hard. It also connects to Claude and ChatGPT, as covered in our guide to to-do apps that work with Claude and ChatGPT.

Goblin Tools

Goblin Tools describes itself as “a collection of small, simple tools, for when things feel too big or complicated.” Magic ToDo breaks a to-do item down into smaller steps, Estimator guesses how long something takes, Compiler turns a brain dump into actions, and a newer Taskmaster tool focuses on one task at a time. Its About page says it is free, that most tools run on AI models from several providers, and that it is mostly designed to help neurodivergent people with overwhelming tasks. Some advanced features need a low-cost Pro subscription, and there are iOS and Android apps. Because text goes to AI models, think before pasting anything private.

What Goblin Tools does better: it offers focused tools for breaking one task into steps, with no setup. It is a set of helpers rather than a day planner, so you can use it alongside one.

Things

Things is a paid, Apple-only task manager. Its features page describes a Today list that shows calendar events beside your to-dos, a separate This Evening list, checklists for breaking a to-do into steps, reminders, and an Upcoming view. It does not mention ADHD. The pricing page sends you to your own App Store for the price, and the Mac version has a free trial.

What Things does better: calm and polish. If a plain, well-made list with a clean Today view is enough for you, it is a pleasure to use. How well it fits depends on how you work.

Is there a free ADHD to-do list app?

Yes, several. Todoist is free for personal use, Structured has a free timeline with an inbox and a focus timer, Tiimo has a free version with a selection of planning tools, and Goblin Tools is free. Free tiers change, so open each app’s store page before relying on one. Start with the free version of whichever approach fits you best: a timeline, a list, or a task breaker. If you are still using it two weeks later, think about paying.

ADHD planner app for iPhone: where one-by.one fits

  • One next step. One card on screen, not a list.

Which ADHD to-do list app should you try first?

  • You want to see the day as a visual timeline: Tiimo or Structured.
  • You are stuck on one big task right now: Goblin Tools.
  • You want one dependable place for everything, free: Todoist.
  • You are on Apple devices and like a clean, quiet list: Things.
  • You would rather have your own AI plan the day and see only the next step: one-by.one, once it is out.

None of these replace talking to a professional. If you think you may have ADHD, the NHS page above explains how to start, and a doctor or a licensed clinician can advise you. An app is a tool, and you are allowed to try several until one feels easy to open.
